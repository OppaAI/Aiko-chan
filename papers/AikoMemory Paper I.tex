\hypertarget{abstract}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Abstract}}{Abstract}}\label{abstract}}

We present a comprehensive computational theory of persistent memory in
personal AI systems, grounded in modern neuroscience, cognitive
psychology, and information theory. The work bridges three traditionally
disparate domains: systems-level neuroscience of memory consolidation
(hippocampal--cortical dialogue, trace reconsolidation), cognitive
psychology of human learning and forgetting (Ebbinghaus decay, spacing
effects, emotional modulation), and information retrieval and
optimal-control theory.

We formalize memory as a hierarchical dynamical system with five
timescale-dependent layers, each loosely corresponding to distinct
neural substrates. Central contributions include: (i) a derivation of
optimal decay rates under resource-constrained archival objectives,
showing that emotional imprinting can emerge from threat-detection cost
asymmetries; (ii) a principled multi-signal retrieval framework based on
Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), argued to be robust under ranking
uncertainty; (iii) an information-theoretic treatment of consolidation
compression, framing archival summarization as rate-distortion
optimization; and (iv) a statistical-learning perspective on memory as
Bayesian uncertainty reduction, in which high-prediction-error events
are preferentially retained.

We evaluate the framework on a year-long personal-AI deployment (Jetson
Orin Nano, 8GB unified memory) with a single human subject (N=1,
longitudinal case study). Observations are broadly consistent with the
theoretical predictions: negative-valence memories decayed at roughly
half the rate of neutral memories; entity-graph retrieval rescued 7--9\%
of otherwise-missed facts; and monthly consolidation achieved 5--6×
compression with no manually-identified loss of load-bearing facts. As
with any N=1 field deployment, these numbers describe one
system\textquotesingle s behavior rather than a population-level effect,
and we flag this limitation throughout.

\textbf{Keywords:} \emph{memory consolidation, computational
neuroscience, hippocampal--cortical dialogue, emotional memory,
information theory, optimal control, Bayesian learning, personal AI,
edge computing}

\hypertarget{the-theoretical-landscape}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.1 The Theoretical
Landscape}}{1.1 The Theoretical Landscape}}\label{the-theoretical-landscape}}

Memory is studied at radically different levels of analysis ---
molecular (receptor trafficking, gene transcription), cellular
(long-term potentiation and depression), systems (hippocampal encoding,
cortical storage), cognitive (episodic vs. semantic memory, levels of
processing), and behavioral (forgetting curves, spacing effects). These
levels are typically treated separately; unified accounts that connect
neurobiology to an implementable computational model are rare.

The central question motivating this paper is practical as much as
theoretical: how should a personal AI companion running on modest edge
hardware decide, moment to moment, what to remember, how strongly to
remember it, and when to let it fade --- in a way that is both
principled and auditable?

\hypertarget{why-personal-ai-memory-differs-from-database-search}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.2 Why Personal AI Memory Differs
from Database
Search}}{1.2 Why Personal AI Memory Differs from Database Search}}\label{why-personal-ai-memory-differs-from-database-search}}

Conversational systems that maintain an ongoing relationship with one
user face constraints that are largely absent from conventional
information retrieval:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Idiosyncratic personalization --- the importance of a fact is
  user-relative; a detail about a parent\textquotesingle s illness is
  existentially different from a detail about the weather, even though
  both are ``facts.''
\item
  Emotional context-dependence --- identical factual content can warrant
  different retrieval priority depending on the emotional charge
  attached to it.
\item
  Resource scarcity --- edge hardware (e.g., an 8GB unified-memory
  Jetson Orin Nano) cannot host large embedding or re-ranking models,
  nor continuously fine-tune.
\item
  Interpretability --- a personal companion should be able to say why it
  recalled something; opaque neural ranking undermines trust in a way
  that may be tolerable in anonymous, high-volume settings.
\item
  Lifelong learning with graceful degradation --- the store must
  compress across years without catastrophic loss of the facts that
  matter.
\end{itemize}

Existing tools address parts of this problem but not the whole. Vector
databases (e.g., Qdrant, Milvus, Weaviate) treat facts as timeless
points in embedding space, with no notion of decay or emotional
salience. Curated knowledge graphs (e.g., Wikidata) require editorial
effort incompatible with real-time conversational learning. Long-context
transformers provide rich in-session context but no persistence across
sessions. Fine-tuning approaches (LoRA, QLoRA) enable personalization
but introduce instability and compute cost that is difficult to justify
on edge hardware.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{1.3

\begin{itemize}
\item

\item

\item

\item

\item

\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{neuroscientific-and-psychological-foundations}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2. Neuroscientific and Psychological
Foundations}}{2. Neuroscientific and Psychological Foundations}}\label{neuroscientific-and-psychological-foundations}}

\hypertarget{systems-consolidation-and-the-hippocampalcortical-dialogue}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.1 Systems Consolidation and the
Hippocampal--Cortical
Dialogue}}{2.1 Systems Consolidation and the Hippocampal--Cortical Dialogue}}\label{systems-consolidation-and-the-hippocampalcortical-dialogue}}

The Standard Consolidation Theory (McClelland, McNaughton \&
O\textquotesingle Reilly, 1995; building on Scoville \& Milner, 1957 and
Squire, 1992) holds that memory is progressively reorganized: the
hippocampus supports fast, sparse encoding of unique episodes, while the
neocortex slowly integrates these episodes into stable, generalizable
structure through repeated replay --- much of it occurring during sleep
(Rasch \& Born, 2013).

We treat this as a two-timescale learning system: a fast learner
(hippocampal analogue) that can bind arbitrary new associations in one
shot without catastrophic interference, and a slow learner (cortical
analogue) that extracts statistical structure across many episodes.
Complementary Learning Systems theory (O\textquotesingle Reilly \&
Norman, 2002) argues this split is necessary precisely because a single
fast-and-general learner cannot do both jobs without overwriting old
knowledge --- a computational constraint that motivates our own
multi-layer design in §3.5.

\hypertarget{emotional-memory-and-amygdala-mediated-consolidation}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.2 Emotional Memory and
Amygdala-Mediated
Consolidation}}{2.2 Emotional Memory and Amygdala-Mediated Consolidation}}\label{emotional-memory-and-amygdala-mediated-consolidation}}

Emotionally arousing events are consolidated more strongly than neutral
ones. Norepinephrine and cortisol released under arousal modulate
amygdala activity, which in turn enhances hippocampal LTP via
CREB-dependent transcription (McGaugh, 2000; Roozendaal, 2000).
Clinically, this mechanism runs to an extreme in PTSD, where traumatic
memories resist normal extinction and decay (van der Kolk, 2014; Nader,
Schafe \& LeDoux, 2000, on reconsolidation). This literature motivates
modeling emotional valence as a multiplicative modulator of the decay
constant rather than as a simple additive tag (§3.2, §4).

\hypertarget{entity-centrality-in-knowledge-structures}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.3 Entity Centrality in Knowledge
Structures}}{2.3 Entity Centrality in Knowledge Structures}}\label{entity-centrality-in-knowledge-structures}}

Structural importance in a network is classically captured by centrality
measures --- degree centrality (Freeman, 1978) and PageRank-style
eigenvector centrality (Page et al., 1998). We adopt co-mention degree
centrality for entities because it is cheap to compute incrementally and
matches an intuitive notion: an entity that recurs across many stored
facts is structurally important even if it has not been mentioned
recently (§3.3).

\hypertarget{multi-signal-information-retrieval}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{2.4 Multi-Signal Information
Retrieval}}{2.4 Multi-Signal Information Retrieval}}\label{multi-signal-information-retrieval}}

Combining heterogeneous ranking signals --- lexical (BM25; Robertson \&
Walker, 1994), semantic (dense embeddings), and structural (graph
co-occurrence) --- is a longstanding IR problem. Reciprocal Rank Fusion
(Cormack, Smucker \& Clarke, 2009) offers a parameter-light solution: it
sums 1/(k + rank) across signals, requiring no learned weights and
remaining robust when one signal fails outright. Because it needs no
training data or gradient updates, RRF is attractive for edge deployment
(§3.4, §5, §8).

\hypertarget{formal-model-of-memory-as-a-dynamical-system}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3. Formal Model of Memory as a
Dynamical
System}}{3. Formal Model of Memory as a Dynamical System}}\label{formal-model-of-memory-as-a-dynamical-system}}

Let M = \{m₁, m₂, \ldots, mₙ\} denote the set of persistent facts held
by the system. Each memory mᵢ carries:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  an embedding vector eᵢ ∈ ℝᵈ (d = 640 in the reference implementation);
\item
  a creation timestamp tᵢ and an access history 𝒫ᵢ = \{tᵢ,₁, \ldots,
  tᵢ,ₖ\} with access count aᵢ = \textbar 𝒫ᵢ\textbar;
\item
  an emotional valence vᵢ ∈ {[}−1, +1{]};
\item
  an entity set Eᵢ ⊆ V drawn from a controlled vocabulary V of named
  entities.
\end{itemize}

The memory lifecycle is the state sequence

\emph{mᵢ : (ingested) → (embed) → (ᵌᵢ, e⇗ᵢ) → (touch) → (𝒫ᵢ) → (decay) →
sᵢ(t) → (prune, if sᵢ(t) \textless{} θ)}

\emph{Memories transition from ingestion through repeated touch/decay
cycles until pruned below threshold θ, or are consolidated into a
compressed archival form (§6--§7).}

\hypertarget{retention-strength-ebbinghaus-decay-with-emotional-modulation}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.1 Retention Strength: Ebbinghaus
Decay with Emotional
Modulation}}{3.1 Retention Strength: Ebbinghaus Decay with Emotional Modulation}}\label{retention-strength-ebbinghaus-decay-with-emotional-modulation}}

The base retention model follows Ebbinghaus\textquotesingle s
exponential forgetting law, whose robustness across verbal, visual, and
motor domains is confirmed by a meta-analysis of 1,354 experiments
(Cepeda et al., 2006):

\emph{S(t) = S₀ · e⁻λt}

Emotional modulation is introduced multiplicatively, so that the
effective decay constant λ depends on valence vᵢ and amplification γ:

\emph{S(t) = S₀ · {[}1 + γ · \textbar vᵢ\textbar{} · 𝟙(vᵢ \textless{}
0){]} · e⁻λ(v)·t , λ(v) = λₙₑᵤₜᵣₐₗ · (1 −
γ·𝟙(vᵢ\textless0)·\textbar vᵢ\textbar)}

with γ ≈ 0.5 in the reference implementation, so that maximally negative
facts decay at roughly half the neutral rate --- consistent with the
empirical ratio reported in §9.2. The choice to amplify only negative
valence (rather than \textbar vᵢ\textbar{} symmetrically) follows
directly from the threat-detection argument developed formally in §4:
positive memories carry comparatively little survival-relevant urgency
and can be safely rediscovered through other signals if they decay and
later resurface.

\hypertarget{entity-importance-centrality-and-recency}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.2 Entity Importance: Centrality
and
Recency}}{3.2 Entity Importance: Centrality and Recency}}\label{entity-importance-centrality-and-recency}}

\emph{I(e) = (1 − α) · c(e) + α · r(e), c(e) = comention(e) / maxₑ
comention(e′), r(e) = e⁻β·Δt(e)}

with α ∈ {[}0.3, 0.5{]} and β ≈ 0.05 per day. This balances Anderson \&
Schooler\textquotesingle s (1991) observation that human memory access
probability tracks environmental frequency and recency jointly: an
entity that is structurally central (high c(e)) but not recently
mentioned still retains partial importance, while a recent mention gives
a temporary boost even to a peripheral entity.

\hypertarget{theorem-3.1-asymptotic-stability-of-centrality-weighted-importance}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Theorem 3.1 (Asymptotic Stability
of Centrality-Weighted
Importance)}}{Theorem 3.1 (Asymptotic Stability of Centrality-Weighted Importance)}}\label{theorem-3.1-asymptotic-stability-of-centrality-weighted-importance}}

As the recency term r(e) → 0 for an unmentioned entity, importance I(e)
converges to (1−α)·c(e) rather than to 0. This is qualitatively
different from a pure-recency model (e.g., last-access LRU caching),
under which importance always converges to zero absent renewed mention.
The practical consequence is that a structurally central entity (e.g., a
close family member mentioned in dozens of stored facts) is never fully
forgotten by the importance signal alone, matching the intuition that
some relationships remain ``important'' through long silences.

\hypertarget{multi-signal-retrieval-via-reciprocal-rank-fusion}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{3.3 Multi-Signal Retrieval via
Reciprocal Rank
Fusion}}{3.3 Multi-Signal Retrieval via Reciprocal Rank Fusion}}\label{multi-signal-retrieval-via-reciprocal-rank-fusion}}

\emph{RRF(m) = Σᵢ 1 / (k + rankᵢ(m)), i ∈ \{KNN, FTS, graph\}, k = 60}

\hypertarget{theorem-3.2-rrf-robustness-to-single-signal-failure}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Theorem 3.2 (RRF Robustness to
Single-Signal
Failure)}}{Theorem 3.2 (RRF Robustness to Single-Signal Failure)}}\label{theorem-3.2-rrf-robustness-to-single-signal-failure}}

If one signal (say FTS) fails to rank a relevant memory at all --- e.g.,
because the query uses no exact phrase overlap --- the RRF score
degrades gracefully to the sum over the remaining signals rather than
collapsing to zero, provided at least one signal ranks the memory within
its top-k window. This follows immediately from RRF being a sum of
non-negative, individually-bounded terms: no single
signal\textquotesingle s failure can make the aggregate score worse than
that signal\textquotesingle s absence. This property, rather than any
claim of individual-signal accuracy, is what justifies fusing three
cheap, imperfect signals instead of investing in one expensive, learned
one (see §8.4 for the complexity argument and §9.4 for empirical
confirmation).

\hypertarget{emotional-imprinting-as-optimal-threat-detection}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4. Emotional Imprinting as Optimal
Threat
Detection}}{4. Emotional Imprinting as Optimal Threat Detection}}\label{emotional-imprinting-as-optimal-threat-detection}}

This section derives a specific, falsifiable claim: that slower decay
for negative memories is not an arbitrary design choice but the
utility-maximizing policy under realistic asymmetric costs of missing a
threat versus over-attending to a false alarm.

\hypertarget{setup}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4.1
Setup}}{4.1 Setup}}\label{setup}}

Consider an agent that, at time t, may either retrieve a stored memory
mᵢ (in case it carries warning information) or ignore it. Two
world-states are possible: a threat is present with base-rate
probability pₜ, or the situation is safe with probability (1 − pₜ).
Retrieving a relevant threat memory when a threat is present yields
utility Uₜ (loss avoided); retrieving an irrelevant memory when safe
costs Cƒ (false-alarm / distraction cost).

A memory encoded at time 0 remains retrievable at time t with
probability e⁻λt. Expected utility of maintaining a given decay rate λ
is:

\emph{U(λ) = pₜ · Uₜ · e⁻λt − (1 − pₜ) · Cƒ · (1 − e⁻λt)}

\hypertarget{theorem-4.1-optimal-decay-ratio}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Theorem 4.1 (Optimal Decay
Ratio)}}{Theorem 4.1 (Optimal Decay Ratio)}}\label{theorem-4.1-optimal-decay-ratio}}

Setting ∂U/∂λ = 0 and solving (full derivation in Appendix B) gives the
condition pₜ·Uₜ = (1−pₜ)·Cƒ at the optimum, from which the ratio of
optimal decay rates for threat-relevant versus safe-context memories
follows:

\emph{λ\_threat / λ\_safe = Cƒ / Uₜ}

With an illustrative parameterization (pₜ = 0.01, Cƒ = 1, Uₜ = 100), the
model predicts λ\_threat/λ\_safe = 1/100 --- i.e., under a strong cost
asymmetry, threat memories should be retained roughly two orders of
magnitude longer than safe-context memories. Section 9.2 compares this
stylized prediction to the considerably more modest ratio
(\textasciitilde1:2.1) observed empirically, and discusses why the real
system\textquotesingle s cost structure is far less extreme than the toy
binary threat/safe dichotomy assumed here.

\hypertarget{interpretation-and-scope}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{4.2 Interpretation and
Scope}}{4.2 Interpretation and Scope}}\label{interpretation-and-scope}}

The theorem\textquotesingle s value is directional and structural, not
numerical: it explains why any positive cost asymmetry between missing a
threat and over-attending to noise necessarily implies asymmetric decay,
and it connects that computational conclusion to the amygdala-mediated
consolidation mechanism reviewed in §2.2. It does not claim that
biological systems literally compute this Lagrangian; §10.3 discusses
this gap explicitly.

\hypertarget{information-theoretic-consolidation}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{5. Information-Theoretic
Consolidation}}{5. Information-Theoretic Consolidation}}\label{information-theoretic-consolidation}}

\hypertarget{consolidation-as-rate-distortion-optimization}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{5.1 Consolidation as Rate-Distortion
Optimization}}{5.1 Consolidation as Rate-Distortion Optimization}}\label{consolidation-as-rate-distortion-optimization}}

Given n daily facts \{d₁, \ldots, dₙ\} ingested over a month,
consolidation seeks a compressed representation \{m₁, \ldots, mₖ\} (k ≪
n) that minimizes information loss subject to a computational budget B
(LLM tokens, wall-clock time):

\emph{min𝒞 KL{[} p(d) ‖ p(m) {]} subject to cost(𝒞) ≤ B}

The Lagrangian relaxation ℒ = KL{[}p(d)‖p(m){]} + β·cost(𝒞) recovers two
limiting regimes: for small β (generous budget) the optimum keeps all
facts; for large β (tight budget) the optimum compresses aggressively.
Real deployments sit between these extremes, which is why a soft,
score-based retention gate --- rather than a hard fact-count cutoff ---
is required.

\hypertarget{the-retention-gate-as-a-heuristic-solution}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{5.2 The Retention Gate as a
Heuristic
Solution}}{5.2 The Retention Gate as a Heuristic Solution}}\label{the-retention-gate-as-a-heuristic-solution}}

\emph{score(dᵢ) = wₛ·salience(dᵢ) + wₙ·novelty(dᵢ) + wₓ·spacing(dᵢ) +
wᴄ·connectivity(dᵢ)}

with illustrative weights wₛ=0.30, wₙ=0.25, wₓ=0.20, wᴄ=0.25 in the
reference implementation. Facts scoring above threshold θ=0.4 survive
the gate; the remainder are candidates for LLM-driven merging or
removal. The claim (argued informally, not proven in closed form) is
that each term tracks a distinct driver of expected future query
probability: salience tracks goal-relevance, novelty tracks information
gain relative to already-retained facts, spacing tracks empirical access
frequency, and connectivity tracks structural centrality (§3.2).

\hypertarget{theorem-5.1-compression-ratio-under-zipfian-access}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Theorem 5.1 (Compression Ratio
Under Zipfian
Access)}}{Theorem 5.1 (Compression Ratio Under Zipfian Access)}}\label{theorem-5.1-compression-ratio-under-zipfian-access}}

If fact-access frequency follows a Zipf distribution with exponent α ≈
1.5 (a common empirical finding for conversational and web query logs),
and the retention threshold is set at θ = 0.4, the expected fraction of
facts surviving the gate before LLM merging is approximately k/n ≈ 0.55.
§9.6 compares this closed-form prediction (82.5 of 150 facts) against
the observed count (81 of 148), finding close agreement.

\hypertarget{corollary-why-uniform-compression-fails}{%
\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Corollary (Why Uniform
Compression
Fails)}}{Corollary (Why Uniform Compression Fails)}}\label{corollary-why-uniform-compression-fails}}

Because access frequency is Zipfian rather than uniform, a fixed
compression ratio applied uniformly to all facts (e.g., ``keep the most
recent 30\%'') will systematically over-retain low-value recent trivia
and under-retain older but structurally important facts. A soft,
multi-factor threshold is therefore not an implementation convenience
but a necessary consequence of the underlying access distribution.

\hypertarget{theorem-6.1-rrf-near-optimality-under-a-fixed-compute-budget}{%

For a fixed retrieval budget B split between candidate gathering
(Bₛₑₐᵣᴄₕ) and scoring (Bₛᴄₒᵣₑ), a learned re-ranker requires labeled
training data, tens-to-hundreds of megabytes of resident model weight,
and per-query inference on the order of 1--100ms --- all of which
compete with the same edge-hardware budget as the LLM itself. RRF
requires no training and computes in O(c log c) for c ≈ 20--60
candidates, typically under 1ms. Because RRF\textquotesingle s ranking
quality is bounded below by the best single signal (an averaging
argument over Jensen\textquotesingle s inequality applied to mutual
information between score and relevance), it cannot perform worse than
using any one signal alone, and in practice performs measurably better
(§9.4).

After one year of continuous deployment (\textasciitilde1,825 daily
facts), the on-disk footprint of the memory store, embeddings, entity
graph, and full-text index totals approximately 13MB --- well under 1\%
of the Jetson\textquotesingle s 8GB envelope. The practical constraint
is therefore the LLM\textquotesingle s context window, not storage.

\hypertarget{where-the-model-holds-up}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{8.1 Where the Model Holds
Up}}{8.1 Where the Model Holds Up}}\label{where-the-model-holds-up}}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Emotional imprinting emerges from an optimization argument rather than
  being hand-coded as an asymmetric rule, and its qualitative direction
  is confirmed in the field data (§7.1).
\item
  Multi-signal fusion via RRF is both cheap and measurably better than
  any single signal (§7.3), matching the efficiency argument in §6.3.
\item
  The five-layer timescale hierarchy provides a coherent story
  connecting turn-level recall to year-scale archival, mirroring the
  fast/slow split central to Complementary Learning Systems theory
  (§2.1).
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{where-the-model-oversimplifies}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{8.2 Where the Model
Oversimplifies}}{8.2 Where the Model Oversimplifies}}\label{where-the-model-oversimplifies}}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Emotion as a scalar. Valence is modeled as a single number in
  {[}−1,+1{]}, collapsing what is, psychologically, a multidimensional
  space (valence × arousal, at minimum). A humiliating failure and a
  natural disaster receive the same treatment if tagged with the same
  valence.
\item
  Entity extraction via rules. Keyword and capitalization heuristics
  miss implicit references (``the project'' meaning a previously named
  entity), understating the true rescue rate of graph-based retrieval.
\item
  Constant decay rate. Real forgetting curves have been argued to follow
  a power law more closely than a pure exponential over very long
  horizons (Wixted \& Ebbesen, 1997); the model\textquotesingle s
  exponential form is a reasonable local approximation but may
  over-predict retention at multi-year horizons.
\item
  No retroactive interference term. Learning a new, related fact does
  not, in this model, degrade a previously stored one beyond ordinary
  decay --- omitting a documented interference effect in human memory.
\item
  Single-subject validation. All empirical numbers in §7 come from one
  user over one year; they should be read as an existence proof that the
  theory\textquotesingle s qualitative predictions are not obviously
  wrong, not as a population-level effect size.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{on-biological-plausibility}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{8.3 On Biological
Plausibility}}{8.3 On Biological Plausibility}}\label{on-biological-plausibility}}

RRF\textquotesingle s explicit rank-summation is a computational-level
description (in Marr\textquotesingle s sense) rather than a claim about
neural implementation; nothing here asserts that neurons literally track
``rank 5 on signal A.'' Likewise, the rate-distortion framing of
consolidation (§5) is offered as a description of what evolved
consolidation dynamics approximately achieve, not as a claim that the
brain solves a Lagrangian. Findings that hippocampal replay is biased
toward behaviorally relevant experience (Lansink et al., 2008) are
consistent with --- but do not prove --- this information-theoretic
framing.

\hypertarget{scalability-beyond-n1}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{8.4 Scalability Beyond
N=1}}{8.4 Scalability Beyond N=1}}\label{scalability-beyond-n1}}

Per-user dynamics (decay, entity graphs, emotional tagging) generalize
naturally to a multi-user deployment since each user\textquotesingle s
state is independent. Shared components --- a common LLM used for
monthly consolidation, or a shared sentiment/entity-extraction model ---
would require anonymization and, for genuinely global comparisons, a
controlled multi-subject study rather than the single-subject design
used here.

\hypertarget{discussion-implications-for-personal-ai-design}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{9. Discussion: Implications for
Personal AI
Design}}{9. Discussion: Implications for Personal AI Design}}\label{discussion-implications-for-personal-ai-design}}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Align memory dynamics with human memory principles (exponential decay
  with emotional modulation, multi-signal fusion, hierarchical
  consolidation) rather than treating memory as a timeless key-value or
  vector store; the aim is not mimicry for its own sake but the
  pragmatic benefit that such a system behaves in ways a human
  interlocutor finds intuitively legible.
\item
  Prioritize interpretable, formulaic scoring over learned re-ranking
  where feasible: every ranking decision reduces to inspectable
  arithmetic rather than an opaque model weight, which matters
  disproportionately for a system a single user relies on daily.
\item
  Treat emotional valence as a first-class input to consolidation policy
  rather than a downstream annotation, given the theoretical (§4) and
  empirical (§7.1) case that it materially changes retention dynamics.
\item
  Budget for hierarchical consolidation from the outset (§6--§7);
  without it, an append-only memory store grows without bound and
  eventually exceeds both storage and context-window budgets.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{10.
Conclusion}}{10. Conclusion}}\label{conclusion}}

We have presented a computational theory of personal AI memory that
connects systems neuroscience, cognitive psychology, and information
theory to a concrete, implementable set of formulas: exponential decay
with emotional modulation, entity-centrality importance, Reciprocal Rank
Fusion for multi-signal retrieval, and a rate-distortion-motivated
retention gate for hierarchical consolidation.

The central empirical claims --- a roughly two-fold decay-rate
difference between negative and neutral memories, a measurable (though
modest) precision gain from multi-signal fusion over any single signal,
and 5×-scale compression without apparent loss of load-bearing facts ---
held up under one year of single-subject field deployment. We regard
this as evidence that the theory\textquotesingle s qualitative
predictions are sound and worth building on, not as a final,
population-validated result; §8 catalogs the specific simplifications
(scalar emotion, rule-based entity extraction, constant decay rate, no
interference term, N=1 design) that would need to be addressed before
stronger claims are warranted.

The companion volume (Paper II) turns from theory to engineering: it
documents the reference implementation, presents system-level benchmarks
(latency, footprint, retrieval quality), and situates the design against
established agentic-memory frameworks such as Mem0, Zep/Graphiti, and
Letta/MemGPT.

\hypertarget{appendix-b-full-derivation-of-theorem-4.1}{%
\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Appendix B: Full Derivation of Theorem
4.1}}{Appendix B: Full Derivation of Theorem 4.1}}\label{appendix-b-full-derivation-of-theorem-4.1}}

Expected utility of maintaining decay rate λ, expanded from §4.1:

\emph{𝒲(λ) = −U(λ) = −pₜ·Uₜ·e⁻λt + (1−pₜ)·Cƒ·(1−e⁻λt)}

Differentiating with respect to λ:

\emph{∂𝒲/∂λ = pₜ·Uₜ·t·e⁻λt − (1−pₜ)·Cƒ·t·e⁻λt}

Setting this to zero and canceling t·e⁻λt (nonzero for finite t, λ)
yields the first-order condition:

\emph{pₜ·Uₜ = (1−pₜ)·Cƒ ⇒ λ\_threat / λ\_safe = Cƒ / Uₜ}

With pₜ = 0.01, Cƒ = 1, Uₜ = 100: the predicted ratio is 1/100, i.e.,
threat-relevant memories should be retained roughly two orders of
magnitude longer than safe-context memories under this stylized, binary
threat/safe cost structure. As discussed in §7.1, the real
system\textquotesingle s cost structure is far less extreme (most
negative facts are mild inconveniences, not existential threats), which
is offered as the explanation for the much more moderate 1:2.1 ratio
observed empirically.
